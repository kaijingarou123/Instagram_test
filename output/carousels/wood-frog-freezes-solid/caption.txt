The wood frog (Lithobates sylvaticus) survives winter with most of its body water frozen, its heart and breathing stopped.

Freezing usually starts when ice touches the frog's skin, and it creeps slowly through the body over many hours. Wild frogs in Alaska carried 13 times more glucose in their muscles than frogs frozen in the lab, possibly built up by repeated freezing and thawing in early autumn.
