Before a dung beetle (Kheper nigroaeneus) rolls its ball, it climbs on top and dances.

The little rotation is an orientation check: researchers found it lets the beetle set a bearing from the sky and roll away in a straight line. On clear moonless nights some dung beetles steer by the Milky Way, the first known use of the Milky Way for orientation by any animal. Filmed in a research arena in South Africa.

Source: Baird et al. 2012, The dung beetle dance: an orientation behaviour?, PLoS ONE; Dacke et al. 2013, Dung beetles use the Milky Way for orientation, Current Biology.

A giraffe (Giraffa camelopardalis) has 7 neck bones, exactly as many as you.

Giraffes, mice, whales and humans all have seven cervical vertebrae; in a giraffe each one is elongated, and big muscles on the upper back hold the neck up. Its prehensile tongue is almost half a metre long, so it can strip leaves about 6 m above the ground, thorns and all. Filmed in the wild in Malawi.

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica, Giraffe; Britannica, One Good Fact: neck bones.

A hummingbird's heart can beat 1,260 times a minute. So how does it survive a cold night?

Torpor. It drops its body temperature and metabolism to a fraction of normal until morning: a nightly mini-hibernation. And a ruby-throated hummingbird can still fatten up and cross 800 km of the Gulf of Mexico in one flight.

Source: Cornell Lab of Ornithology (All About Birds). Photos: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 (credits shown on screen).

The common kingfisher (Alcedo atthis) owes its famous blue to structure, not pigment.

Under an electron microscope, its cyan back and blue tail feathers contain spongy nanostructures that scatter blue light and make it shimmer as the angle changes. The orange breast feathers work the ordinary way, with small pigment granules.

Source: Stavenga et al. 2011, Kingfisher feathers: colouration by pigments, spongy nanostructures and thin films, Journal of Experimental Biology 214.

The leopard (Panthera pardus) is a big cat that stores its meals in trees.

An agile climber, it drags kills up into the branches, out of reach of scavengers, and may go on hunting even while several carcasses are already cached. Its preferred prey weighs about 10 to 40 kg. This one was filmed in Rajaji Tiger Reserve, Uttarakhand, India.

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica, Leopard; Animal Diversity Web, Panthera pardus.

Orcas in the Antarctic Peninsula have a hunting technique that looks like teamwork from a movie: they line up, charge an ice floe and dive at the last second, creating a wave that washes seals off the ice.

It's culture, not instinct. Only some pods do it, and young orcas take years to learn.

Source: Pitman & Durban (2012), Marine Mammal Science. Photos: Callan Carpenter via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 / CC BY 4.0.

The Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus) displays a train, not a tail.

The train is mostly the bird's upper tail coverts, grown enormously long and each tipped with an eyespot. The true tail lies beneath it: in courtship the peacock lifts his tail to raise and fan the train, then vibrates it so the feathers shimmer and rustle. Filmed at the Botanical Garden in Hyderabad, India.

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica, Peacock.

snow-leopard-no-roarApproved, queued

21.5 s · generated ambient bed (CC0-equivalent) · format:myth-bust format:fact-over-footage tactic:hook-text

Snow leopards can't roar. Lions, tigers, jaguars and leopards have a pad of fibro-elastic tissue in the larynx that lets them rumble; the snow leopard doesn't, so it chuffs, growls and yowls instead.

The 'ghost of the mountains' lives at 3,000–4,500 m across 12 countries. Fewer than 10,000 mature adults remain (IUCN: Vulnerable).

Sources: Hast (1989) J. Anatomy; IUCN Red List 2017; Snow Leopard Trust. Photos: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 (captive animals, Zoo Karlsruhe); credits on screen.

A spider wasp (family Pompilidae) does not kill the tarantula it hunts.

She stings it in the nerve ganglia under the thorax, which paralyses it, drags it to a burrow, lays one egg on its body and seals the entrance. The larva then feeds on the spider while it is still alive. There are about 5,000 species worldwide; the best known are the tarantula hawks of the southwestern United States.

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica, Spider wasp.
